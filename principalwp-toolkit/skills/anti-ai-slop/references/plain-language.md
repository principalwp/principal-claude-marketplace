# Plain language

Coined names, invented compounds, and insider shorthand break plain language even when they are not AI tells; a sentence of plain words can still be unreadable at six clauses deep. Two parts over the same text: a **census** of every coined term, and a **readability sweep** of every sentence. A term can pass one and fail the other.

Set the reader first: the piece's actual audience. A term is fine only if it lands cold for that reader. A name real to the writer but coined for this audience still gets flagged.

## Part 1. The coined-term census

A census, not an exception list: enumerate every term you check and show its verdict, so nothing passes silently.

1. Go block by block. A block is the lede, a paragraph, a heading, a list item; on repeating structures (cards, rows, bullets), every one is its own block.
2. In each block, enumerate every term a reader must already know to follow the sentence: coined names, invented compound nouns and nominalizations ("the source-blind sufficiency pass," "read-but-not-synthesized failure"), imported jargon used as if shared ("confound," "forensics"), bare abbreviations, terse insider shorthand.
3. Verdict per term:
   - **OK** means plain English, a general term the reader knows, or a name that plainly predates this piece for this reader. No action.
   - **COINED** means an invented name, a nominalization, or imported jargon, whether or not the piece defines it. Mark **naked** (not even glossed, which is worst) or **defined** (glossed but still coined). Both get a plain rewrite.

**Defining jargon does not make prose plain; replacing it does.** A glossed coinage still gets rewritten. The gloss only lowers its severity. Before any coined term, ask whether a plain phrase carries it with no gloss at all: "joins their returns into its candidate ledger" → "merges what they found into its running list of candidates." The smell-words are illustrations, not a banned list. A real feature genuinely named "ledger," once defined, can be fine; the test is whether *this* reader needs a gloss to follow the sentence.

Fix mode: apply every rewrite; the census stays an internal working note. Detect mode: the census is the output, listing each coined term, a quoted sentence, and the rewrite.

## Part 2. The readability sweep

Same blocks, judging sentences, not words. One idea per sentence, roughly 25 words, ceiling around 35; four facts are four sentences, not one semicolon chain. Keep a subject next to its verb. Flag, quoting the sentence and giving the rewrite:

- **[long]** means over ~35 words, or three-plus clauses, or chained on semicolons.
- **[apart]** means subject and verb held apart by a long appositive or relative clause.
- **[order]** means point-last ordering where point-first reads clearer.
- **[opaque]** marks a sentence whose meaning lands only if you already know the subject.
- **[bulk]** marks a block too long to hold in one read; say roughly how much has to go, but never cut a fact a decision rests on.

The tags are illustrations, not a checklist: a sentence that stops you but matches no tag is still a finding. Genuinely short, plain prose earns few findings. That is a pass, not a skipped sweep.
